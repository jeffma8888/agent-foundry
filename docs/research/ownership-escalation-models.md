# Decision rights and human escalation in orgs and agent systems

Autonomous multi-agent organizations face a problem human companies solved decades ago: who is allowed to decide what, who must be consulted, and which choices are dangerous enough to stop and escalate. This brief surveys the mature human frameworks, extracts what makes them work, and translates them into machine-checkable rules for an always-on agent development org where every role activation costs a model call and human attention is the scarcest resource.

## Decision-rights frameworks: RACI, DACI, RAPID (Evidence)

Three named frameworks dominate. **RACI** tags each task with Responsible (does the work), Accountable (single owner of the outcome), Consulted (two-way input), and Informed (one-way notification). **DACI** reframes the same idea around one decision: a Driver corrals stakeholders and drives to a dated decision, exactly one Approver makes the call ("yes: one!"), Contributors have "a voice, but not a vote," and the Informed have "no vote, no voice" ([Atlassian Team Playbook](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci)). Atlassian positions DACI for complex, high-stakes, cross-functional and resource-allocation decisions, and frames the Approver as an active decision-maker rather than a passive "rubber stamp" ([Atlassian Team Playbook](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci)).

**RAPID**, created by Bain, splits the roles differently: Recommend drives the proposal and gathers input (only one recommender); Agree must sign off that the recommendation is feasible and meets "mandatory requirements, such as legal or regulatory requirements" (assigned sparingly); Perform executes; Input supplies expertise; and Decide "makes the final decision and commits the organization to action" ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/)). Bain notes that if Recommend and Agree cannot reconcile, the Decide role breaks the tie, and advises starting with "high-value or high-frequency decisions" rather than formalizing roles for everything ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/)).

The frameworks differ most on the veto. RACI and DACI have no built-in blocking role; RAPID's Agree is a hard gate for legal and regulatory constraints. All three converge on one principle: a single accountable decider. DACI is blunt ("one!"); RAPID says "ideally, there should only be one decider," and if a group must decide, it demands the tie-break rule be defined up front ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/)).

## Amazon: one-way vs two-way door decisions (Evidence)

Amazon's public model separates decisions by reversibility. In Bezos's 2016 shareholder letter, "many decisions are reversible, two-way doors" and "can use a light-weight process ... so what if you're wrong?", while irreversible choices deserve deliberation ([Amazon, 2016](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)). The letter pairs this with three velocity rules: decide at roughly "70% of the information you wish you had"; use "disagree and commit" when there is conviction without consensus; and "recognize true misalignment issues early and escalate them immediately," because the default alternative is resolution "by exhaustion" where "whoever has more stamina carries the decision" ([Amazon, 2016](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)). Escalation here is not failure; it is the designed fast path when parties are fundamentally unaligned.

## How startups define founder-approval-required categories (Evidence + Synthesis)

**Evidence.** Corporate governance separates authority that can be pushed down from accountability that cannot. Delegation theory holds that "while a manager can delegate authority for a task, the ultimate responsibility is not transferred"; the "principle of absoluteness of responsibility" states that responsibility itself cannot be delegated, and the "parity of authority and responsibility" principle requires the two to match ([Wikipedia: Delegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delegation_of_authority)). Startups operationalize this as a delegation-of-authority matrix (spend thresholds, contract sign-off limits) plus a short list of reserved matters that always require founder or board approval.

**Synthesis (opinion).** The reserved list is remarkably consistent across companies and maps cleanly onto RAPID's Agree gate and DACI's single Approver. It typically covers raising or spending above a threshold, signing contracts that bind the company, anything touching equity or fundraising, hiring or firing senior roles, legal settlements, and public statements made in the company's name. The common thread is irreversibility and external bindingness: Amazon's one-way doors by another name.

## Human-in-the-loop gate design for AI agent systems (Evidence)

Anthropic treats human oversight as a first-class design element, not an afterthought. Agents "begin their work with either a command from, or interactive discussion with, the human user," then "plan and operate independently, potentially returning to the human for further information or judgement," and "can then pause for human feedback at checkpoints or when encountering blockers" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Two mechanisms matter for gating: programmatic checks (the "gate" in a prompt chain that verifies intermediate output before proceeding) and sectioning guardrails, where "one model instance processes user queries while another screens them" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Anthropic also recommends stopping conditions such as a maximum number of iterations "to maintain control," extensive sandbox testing, and, for coding agents, that "human review remains crucial for ensuring solutions align with broader system requirements" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). The stated principle is to keep the loop simple and transparent, "explicitly showing the agent's planning steps," so a reviewer can actually audit a decision at the gate.

## Which categories must escalate, and how to make triggers deterministic (Synthesis / opinion)

Opinion, grounded in the evidence above. Five categories should always escalate to the human owner because they are one-way doors with external blast radius: **security** (credentials, auth, access control), **privacy and personal data** (any PII), **spending** (real money or paid API commitments), **legal exposure** (contracts, licenses, IP, factual claims about third parties), and **public visibility** (anything published under the org's name). These are exactly the reserved matters a founder keeps, and exactly the RAPID Agree gate for "legal or regulatory requirements" ([Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/)).

The engineering trick is to make the trigger machine-checkable, not judgment-based. A human-worded rule ("escalate risky changes") is non-deterministic; a policy-as-code rule is deterministic. Open Policy Agent demonstrates the pattern: software "queries OPA and supplies structured data (e.g., JSON) as input," and OPA evaluates it against declarative rules to return an allow/deny (or richer) decision ([Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/)). Concretely, an agent org can encode escalation as file-and-diff predicates the release gate evaluates every iteration: a changed path matching a secrets or auth glob, a diff that adds a network call or a new dependency, a diff touching a license or legal file, a spend estimate above a numeric threshold, or any push that would flip a private repository to public. Each predicate is a boolean over the diff and the environment: reproducible, loggable, and impossible for an agent to argue past. OPA's model of decoupling the policy decision from its enforcement maps directly: the gate role computes the decision; a dumb wrapper enforces the stop ([Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/)).

## Catching exaggerated impact claims: calibration, pre-mortems, red teams (Evidence)

Three techniques from decision science attack over-optimistic claims.

**Forecast calibration.** The Good Judgment research program found that accuracy gains come from measurable, trainable behaviors: roughly "50% ... attributed to noise reduction ... The remaining 25% is information improvement ... and that last 25% will be then bias reduction" ([Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/)). A related 2025 study assessed and combatted "overconfidence among 2,000 national security officials" ([Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/)). The practical lever: force probabilistic estimates with explicit confidence, then score them against outcomes over time so overclaiming is caught by track record, not opinion.

**Pre-mortem.** Gary Klein's technique has the team "assume that the project they are planning has just failed ... and then generate plausible" reasons, which surfaces reservations that people are otherwise "reluctant to speak up about ... during the all-important planning phase" ([Klein, HBR 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)). It is a cheap, structured way to convert silent doubt into recorded risk before commitment.

**Red-team review.** Microsoft frames red teaming as "emulating real-world adversaries and their tools, tactics, and procedures to identify risks, uncover blind spots, validate assumptions." For AI specifically, red teaming "focuses on failures from both malicious and benign personas," must run "multiple rounds" because generative systems are probabilistic ("running the same input twice may provide different outputs"), and pairs with "systematic, automated measurement" and defense in depth ([Microsoft, 2023](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2023/08/07/microsoft-ai-red-team-building-future-of-safer-ai/)). Microsoft's commitment that "all high-risk AI systems will go through independent red teaming before deployment" underscores that the reviewer must be independent of the builder ([Microsoft, 2023](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2023/08/07/microsoft-ai-red-team-building-future-of-safer-ai/)).

These map onto a dedicated product-gate role: calibration disciplines the impact number, the pre-mortem forces the failure story, and independent red-team review stress-tests both.

## Implications for an autonomous AI-agent dev org

- **One accountable decider per decision, encoded in files.** DACI and RAPID both insist on a single decider ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci), [Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/)). Give each product decision exactly one gate role that writes its verdict to a tracked file; never let two roles co-own a call, since agents cannot resolve ties "by exhaustion" and will loop.
- **Split decisions by reversibility to save model calls.** Two-way-door changes (internal refactor, docs, test tweaks) should auto-approve through cheap programmatic checks; reserve expensive human escalation and multi-role review for one-way doors ([Amazon, 2016](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)). Every avoided role activation is a saved model call from the shared budget.
- **Make escalation triggers deterministic diff predicates, not prose.** Encode the five reserved categories (security, PII, spend, legal, public visibility) as policy-as-code booleans over the diff and environment ([Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/)); a leak-guard that blocks a public push on a banned token is exactly this pattern. Prose rules ("escalate if risky") are non-deterministic and unauditable.
- **The release gate is the only role that touches irreversible actions.** Concentrate pushes, publishes, and spend commitments in one final gate that re-runs every deterministic check itself; because roles keep no memory between iterations except files and git, the gate must not trust upstream claims and must recompute from ground truth.
- **Add a feasibility/impact gate armed with calibration plus pre-mortem.** Require every impact claim to carry a probability and a one-line pre-mortem failure story ([Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/), [Klein, HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)); reject claims that cannot state how they would be measured. This is a cheap, single-run check that catches the most common failure of enthusiastic agent output.
- **Independent red-team review, run more than once.** The reviewer or tester must be a separate run from the builder, operate spec-only, and (given probabilistic output) sample more than once before signing off ([Microsoft, 2023](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2023/08/07/microsoft-ai-red-team-building-future-of-safer-ai/)). Independence is structural, not a prompt instruction.
- **Bound autonomy with hard stopping conditions.** Cap iterations and require a human checkpoint at reserved-category boundaries ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)); an always-on org must default to "stop and escalate" on any deterministic reserved-category hit, because human attention is the bottleneck and should be spent only where the door is one-way.
- **Log every gate decision as structured data.** OPA-style, emit the decision plus the inputs that produced it ([Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/)) so a human can audit in seconds; transparency of the planning trail is what makes rare human review fast enough to be feasible ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)).
